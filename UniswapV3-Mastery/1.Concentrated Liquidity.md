### 1. What Are Reserves?
**Reserves** are the amounts of each token held by a liquidity pool.

- `x` = reserve of token X.
- `y` = reserve of token Y.

**Example:** A pool holds 10 ETH and 20,000 USDC.
- `x = 10 ETH`
- `y = 20,000 USDC`

### 2. Why Do Reserves Matter?
In Uniswap V2, the ratio of reserves determines the spot price, while swaps change the reserve balances and therefore change the price.

**Example:**



### 3. Reserves vs. Liquidity
- **Reserves:** Actual token amounts held by the pool.
- **Liquidity:** In V2's constant-product model, the product of the reserves is represented by `k`.

\[
x \times y = k
\]

**Example:**

\[
k = 10 \times 20,000 = 200,000
\]

In Uniswap V2, swaps approximately preserve `k`, excluding the effect of trading fees and other implementation details.

**Key takeaway:** Reserves represent token quantities; `k` represents the constant-product relationship between those quantities.


# Liquidity

**Liquidity** represents the pool's capacity to facilitate token swaps. In Uniswap V2, it is related to the product of the two token reserves.

### 1. Constant-Product Formula

\[
x.y = k
\]

- `x` = reserve of token0
- `y` = reserve of token1
- `k` = product of reserves

### 2. Example

Suppose a pool contains:

```text
1 ETH × 1,000 USDC = 1,000 (k)
```

Another reserve combination:

```text
1.1111 ETH × 900 USDC ≈ 1,000 (k)
```

Both combinations have approximately the same `k`.

**Key point:** Different reserve combinations can have the same `k`. A larger `k` generally indicates a larger pool when comparing equivalent token units.

### 3. Liquidity and Reserves

When LPs deposit tokens, the pool's reserves increase, generally increasing `k`.

A larger `k` moves the constant-product curve farther from the origin, indicating greater reserves.

Remember:
**Uniswap V2:** `k = x × y` — the product of the pool's token reserves.

**Uniswap V3:** `L` — the liquidity used to calculate token amounts and price movement within a selected price range.

**Main difference:** V2 uses a constant-product model across the price curve, while V3 lets liquidity providers concentrate liquidity within chosen price ranges.

## Liquidity and Price Impact

Higher liquidity → lower price impact.

Lower liquidity → higher price impact.

Important: Price impact depends on the trade size relative to the pool's active liquidity, not just the total amount of tokens held by the pool.


## Price of an Asset in an AMM

In Uniswap V2, the price of `token0` in terms of `token1` is calculated using the reserve ratio:



**Example:**

```text
token0 = 2 ETH
token1 = 4,000 USDC

Price of token0 = 4,000 / 2
                = 2,000 USDC per ETH
```

### How Does Price Change?

- `token0` reserve decreases → `token0` price increases.
- `token0` reserve increases → `token0` price decreases.

**Why?** When fewer units of `token0` remain in the pool relative to `token1`, each unit of `token0` becomes more expensive in terms of `token1`.

**Remember:** In V2, the reserve ratio determines the spot price. In V3, the pool tracks price using `sqrtPriceX96`.

## Price as the angle from the origin

- **Same ray = Same price:** In Uniswap V2, price = `reserve(token1) / reserve(token0)`. If both reserves change proportionally, the ratio and price remain constant.

- **Angle = Price:** Imagine the reserves plotted on a graph, with `token0` on the X-axis and `token1` on the Y-axis. A steeper ray from the origin means a higher `token0` price in terms of `token1`.

  
**Example:** `1 ETH / 2,000 USDC` and `2 ETH / 4,000 USDC` both give a price of `2,000 USDC per ETH`. They lie on the same ray and have the same price, even though the reserve amounts differ.


## Price Impact

**Price impact** is the change in a token's price caused by a trade in an AMM.

### How It Works

When a user buys `token0` using `token1`:

- `token0` reserve decreases.
- `token1` reserve increases.
- The price of `token0` increases.

In Uniswap V2:


**Example:**
- Small trade → Small price impact.
- Large trade → Generally larger price impact.

**Key Takeaway:** Every nonzero swap changes the pool's price. Higher active liquidity generally means lower price impact for the same trade.

## Price Impact at Different Liquidity Levels

**Key principle:** For the same trade, higher liquidity results in lower price impact.

### Example 1: Low Liquidity
- Reserves: 10 USDC and 10 USDT
- `k = 10 × 10 = 100`
- Buy 1 USDC → Pay approximately 1.11 USDT.
- Price increases from 1 to 1.234 USDT per USDC.
- **Price impact: 23.4%**

### Example 2: Higher Liquidity
- Reserves: 100 USDC and 100 USDT
- `k = 100 × 100 = 10,000`
- Buy 1 USDC → Pay approximately 1.01 USDT.
- Price increases from 1 to 1.02 USDT per USDC.
- **Price impact: 2%**

### Key Takeaway
The same trade causes greater price impact in a pool with lower liquidity and less price impact in a pool with higher liquidity.

## Capital-Efficient Liquidity

**Capital efficiency** means using less capital to provide higher effective liquidity where most trading is expected to happen.

### How It Works
- Traders want high liquidity to reduce price impact.
- LPs want to provide less capital instead of depositing huge amounts.
- Example: USDC and USDT are expected to trade near a **1:1 price** because both are dollar-pegged stablecoins.
- Most trades are therefore expected to happen near this price.

### Problem with Uniswap V2
Uniswap V2 spreads liquidity across a price curve ranging from nearly zero to infinity.

Much of that liquidity may be inefficiently allocated to extreme prices where little trading is expected.

### Key Takeaway
**Uniswap V3 improves capital efficiency by allowing LPs to concentrate liquidity within selected price ranges instead of spreading it across the entire curve.**

## Concentrating Liquidity

**Concentrated liquidity** means allocating more liquidity to the price range where most trades are expected to happen, instead of spreading it across the entire price curve.

### Example: USDC/USDT Pool

Both stablecoins are expected to trade near a **1:1 price**.

Suppose an LP selects a price range:

- **Lower bound:** 0.99 USDT per USDC
- **Upper bound:** 1.01 USDT per USDC

Within this range, the pool has higher liquidity, reducing price impact for trades.

### How It Works
- **Within the selected range:** Higher liquidity → lower price impact.
- **Outside the selected range:** Liquidity may be much lower, causing higher price impact.
- LPs can use less capital to provide effective liquidity where trading is most likely.

### Trade-Off
If the price moves outside the selected range, the LP's liquidity becomes inactive and stops earning swap fees until the price returns to that range.

### Key Takeaway
**Uniswap V3 improves capital efficiency by concentrating liquidity within chosen price ranges. However, selecting a range that is too narrow increases the risk of becoming inactive when the market price moves.**


## How Uniswap V3 Implements Concentrated Liquidity

### 1. Different Liquidity Ranges
- LPs choose specific price ranges where they want to provide liquidity.
- Different ranges can have different liquidity levels.
- Higher liquidity within a range generally means lower price impact for swaps in that range.

### 2. How Does It Work?
- The article uses multiple `x × y = k` curves to visualize different liquidity levels.
- A larger `k` represents a curve farther from the origin, assuming equivalent token units.
- The price can move smoothly between price ranges, while active liquidity changes at range boundaries.

**Important:** In actual Uniswap V3, `L` represents liquidity. The `k` values are a simplified visualization, not the protocol's actual liquidity variable.

### 3. What Are Ticks?
- **Ticks** are predefined price boundaries.
- LPs select lower and upper ticks for their liquidity positions.
- When the price crosses a tick, the protocol updates active liquidity according to the positions entering or leaving the active range.

### Key Takeaway
**Uniswap V2 spreads liquidity across the entire price curve, while Uniswap V3 allows LPs to concentrate liquidity within selected price ranges using ticks.**

# Uniswap V3 Invariant — Piecewise Curve

## 1. What Is the Piecewise Invariant?

- \(x\) = Token0 reserve quantity in the simplified model.
- \(y\) = Token1 reserve quantity in the simplified model.
- \(p\) = current price.
- \(tick_i\) = price boundaries.
- \(k_i\) = illustrative constant-product parameter for each segment.

**Main idea:** Different price ranges can have different amounts of liquidity, so the visualization represents them using different \(k_i\) values.

## 2. Why Does Each Segment Have a Different k?

In the interactive graph, changing a segment's \(k\) changes the position of its curve relative to the origin.

For example:

- \(k_1=6\)
- \(k_2=10\)
- \(k_3=14\)
- \(k_4=14\)
- \(k_5=13\)
- \(k_6=11\)

These are demonstration values used by the visualization, **not actual Uniswap V3 liquidity values**.

They help illustrate that liquidity conditions can differ across price ranges.

## 3. How Can Price Move Between Curves That Do Not Touch?

The graph draws separate curve segments to represent different price intervals.

Although the curves may appear disconnected visually, that does not mean the actual pool's price must jump between disconnected curves.

In real Uniswap V3:
1. The pool tracks the current price and active liquidity.
2. A swap moves the price through the current liquidity range.
3. When the price crosses a tick, the pool updates active liquidity.
4. The swap continues using the updated liquidity, provided liquidity is available.

If there is no active liquidity through the next price interval, a swap cannot simply pass through that interval without liquidity being supplied.

## 4. Is This the Actual Uniswap V3 Formula?

**No. The piecewise \(xy=k_i\) formula is a simplified teaching model.**

Uniswap V3 does not implement the pool as a collection of separate constant-product curves, each governed by its own stored \(k_i\).

Instead, it uses:
- \(L\): the liquidity variable.
- `sqrtPriceX96`: the square-root price representation.
- Ticks: discrete price boundaries where active liquidity may change.

The piecewise formula is useful for understanding the *idea* of liquidity varying across price ranges, but it is not the protocol's literal invariant.

## 5. Why Is This Model Useful?

It helps explain the key difference between Uniswap V2 and V3:

- **V2:** Liquidity is distributed across the entire price curve.
- **V3:** Liquidity providers can concentrate liquidity within selected price ranges.

As the price crosses ticks, the active liquidity can change, allowing different liquidity conditions at different prices.

### Final Takeaway

**Treat \(xy=k_i\) as a conceptual visualization, not the actual Uniswap V3 implementation.** The important concept is that V3's active liquidity changes as the price moves through tick boundaries.

# Uniswap V3 — Liquidity Distribution and Tick Crossing

## 1. Liquidity Distribution Across Price Ranges

In Uniswap V3, LPs choose a **lower and upper price boundary** for each liquidity position. Only positions covering the current price contribute active liquidity.

In the RareSkills conceptual model:

\[
xy=k_i
\]

- Different \(k_i\) values illustrate different liquidity conditions across price segments.
- Multiple segments may have the same \(k_i\).
- \(k_i=0\) represents a range with no liquidity in this simplified model.

**Note:** This piecewise formula is illustrative. Actual V3 uses liquidity \(L\), not separate stored \(k_i\) values.

LPs tend to concentrate liquidity around prices where they expect trading because they earn swap fees when their active liquidity is used.

## 2. Ticks and Price Boundaries

**Ticks** represent predefined price levels where liquidity positions can begin or end. Valid boundaries are restricted by the pool's tick spacing, which depends on its fee tier.

When the price crosses a tick, the pool updates active liquidity according to the positions starting or ending there.

## 3. Stablecoin Liquidity Concentration

For a USDC/USDT pool, the expected price is approximately:


Using the conceptual reserve model:



When \(x=y\), \(P=1\). LPs may concentrate liquidity around this price because they expect substantial trading near the 1:1 ratio.

More active liquidity generally reduces price impact for a given trade.

## 4. What Happens When Price Moves Outside a Range?

Suppose an LP provides liquidity between $1,900 and $2,100.

- **Price within the range:** The position contributes active liquidity and may earn swap fees.
- **Price above $2,100 or below $1,900:** The position becomes inactive and stops earning swap fees.

LPs may manually remove and reposition liquidity around a new market price. This is optional and may incur transaction costs.

## 5. Tick Crossing and Price Continuity

Although the conceptual graph shows separate curve segments, actual swaps can move across tick boundaries without requiring a discontinuous price jump.

When a tick is crossed:
1. The pool updates active liquidity.
2. The swap continues using the updated liquidity.
3. If no liquidity is available in the next price interval, the swap cannot proceed through that interval.

**Important:** A tick crossing updates active liquidity; it does not itself deposit or withdraw tokens from the pool.

## 6. Uniswap V2 vs V3

| Feature | Uniswap V2 | Uniswap V3 |
|---|---|---|
| Liquidity distribution | Across the full price curve | Within LP-selected price ranges |
| Price boundaries | No LP-defined ranges | Tick-based boundaries |
| Active liquidity | Follows the V2 pool model | Changes as price crosses ticks |
| Price impact | Depends on pool liquidity and trade size | Depends on active liquidity and trade size |
| LP management | No range repositioning required | LPs may need to reposition ranges |

Both protocols allow LPs to add/remove liquidity, traders to swap tokens, and users to access price-related oracle functionality.

### Key Takeaway

**Uniswap V3 improves capital efficiency by concentrating liquidity within selected price ranges. Ticks define range boundaries, and active liquidity changes as the price crosses them.**
